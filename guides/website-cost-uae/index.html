How much does a website cost in the UAE?

A useful website budget begins with scope, not one number. A five-page business site, a catalogue with hundreds of products and a platform with customer accounts are different projects. Here's how to compare the real cost of each before approaching a developer.

By Mohammad Sayyad Shafan·28 November 2026

What published UAE prices actually tell you

There is no single standard website price in the UAE. To get a sense of the market, I reviewed three public UAE provider price pages on 28 November 2026. The figures below are examples of advertised packages, not an independent market average, a quotation from SHFN or a price guarantee.

Alo Development listed AED 3,000 for a currently discounted starter site based on one of its existing designs. DevelopPrism advertised a WordPress starter project from AED 3,500 and a broader business website from AED 8,500. Darb listed a bilingual business site at AED 4,500. Those are three different scopes, so the numbers aren't interchangeable.

For e-commerce, Darb showed AED 8,000 and DevelopPrism showed a starting price of AED 15,000. Their packages describe different product, language, payment and delivery requirements. For custom platforms, DevelopPrism listed a starting point of AED 30,000, Alo showed a promotional AED 41,250 for its product build, and Darb listed custom web apps from AED 49,000.

These figures can change. Some published prices exclude VAT or hosting, while others bundle an initial hosting period. Verify the provider's current page and written quotation rather than treating any example as a fixed market tariff.

Choose the project type before asking for a price

A landing page is the simplest option when the business has one offer and one clear action. A company website needs a broader structure: services, about information, proof of work, contact details and pages written around the questions customers actually ask.

A product catalogue can be more useful than a full online store for businesses that sell on quotation, configure products for each job or need a sales conversation before the price is agreed. A real online store needs additional work for payment, stock, delivery, order records and customer communication.

A custom web application is different again. User accounts, permissions, databases, workflows, integrations and reporting bring more development and testing effort, even if there are relatively few visible screens.

The six decisions that change the budget

  • Design: adapting a proven visual direction is different from creating a distinct interface, custom motion or interactive 3D from scratch.
  • Content: decide who supplies final service descriptions, images, product details, translations and brand guidelines. Missing content often delays launch.
  • Languages: Arabic and English need a proper language structure and right-to-left layout review, not only translated text.
  • Product and data scale: a few items can be entered manually, while a large catalogue may need structured imports, searchable attributes and a clear update process.
  • Functionality: a WhatsApp enquiry link is simpler than a contact system with notifications, a login area, payment gateway or integration with an existing business tool.
  • Quality and handover: responsive alignment, accessibility, performance checks, deployment, security basics and documented access all take time and should appear in the scope.

Separate the build from ongoing costs

Ask which items are one-time and which recur. Domain renewal, hosting, business email, premium themes or plugins, storage, video delivery, payment provider fees and third-party subscriptions are not automatically included in development.

Maintenance is another decision: who applies updates, checks backups, renews services and fixes issues after launch? A static company site may need little ongoing infrastructure, while an online store or custom application needs a defined operational plan.

You should retain access to the domain, hosting and business accounts. Ask the developer to explain what is included at handover and what can be changed without returning to the development team.

A practical example: planning a small business website

Imagine a Sharjah-based service business that needs a homepage, three individual service pages, an about page and a contact page. It has a logo, service descriptions and photos, but wants a better mobile experience and a clear WhatsApp enquiry button.

A reasonable brief would specify those pages, the desired visual direction, English or bilingual content, contact paths, basic search metadata, mobile testing and the owner-held hosting account. If staff rarely change the site, a simple static implementation may be sufficient; if they regularly publish updates, a content management system may be more worthwhile.

The brief becomes larger only when the business adds requirements such as bookings, an editable product catalogue, customer accounts or payments. Those should be quoted separately instead of hidden in a vague website package.

What to ask for before accepting a quotation

A low quote is not necessarily a bad quote and a high quote is not automatically better. The important difference is whether it covers the website your business needs and whether the responsibilities are clear.

  • A page list and the functions included on each page.
  • Who is responsible for copy, images, translation, product data and legal policies.
  • The number of design review rounds and the acceptance criteria for launch.
  • A clear list of excluded items and ongoing third-party charges.
  • Responsive, security and performance checks, and how issues will be handled.
  • Domain, hosting, source files, CMS access and the handover plan.
